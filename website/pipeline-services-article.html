AI Is Writing More Code Than Ever. Who Is Preserving the Architecture?

As AI accelerates development, the real advantage goes to systems that stay clear, structured, and easy to change.

AI is changing software development at an extraordinary pace.

Code that once took days or weeks to write can now be produced in minutes. Entire components can be scaffolded quickly. Boilerplate can disappear. Refactors can happen faster. Even complex ideas can be translated into working code with far less friction than before.

I believe this is one of the most powerful shifts our industry has ever seen.

But I also believe it introduces a new architectural risk that many teams are underestimating.

The real problem is not simply whether AI-generated code works.

The real problem is whether the resulting system remains understandable, maintainable, and easy to evolve.

A system can be functionally correct and still be structurally fragile.

It can pass tests and still become painful to reason about. It can move fast today and quietly accumulate the kind of complexity that slows every future feature down.

As AI writes more of our systems, architecture matters more than ever.

The new risk is structural drift at machine speed

When people talk about AI-generated code, the conversation often centers on correctness. Will it produce bugs? Will it hallucinate? Will it miss edge cases?

Those are valid concerns, but they are not the only ones, and in many cases they are not the most dangerous ones.

The deeper risk is structural drift.

These motivations existed for me long before AI. I have seen firsthand what happens when large systems grow without a clear architectural structure. They become a nightmare to maintain. One person ends up carrying most of the system in their head, and everyone else depends on that person for answers. Documentation becomes stale because the real knowledge lives in tribal memory, not in the architecture itself. Issues start happening on a weekly basis, and even small feature work feels dangerous because the execution flow is no longer obvious. AI did not invent this problem. It simply makes it easier for teams to scale the problem faster if they are not careful.

When code is produced quickly and repeatedly, especially across large systems, complexity can spread faster than teams can manage it. Logic ends up split across too many helpers, wrappers, classes, services, callbacks, utilities, and generated fragments. The system still runs, but the flow becomes harder to see.

That is when maintainability starts to collapse.

A maintainable system is not just one that works. It is one that people can understand. It is one that engineers can extend confidently. It is one where adding a new feature does not feel like surgery.

In the AI era, this matters even more because software is no longer limited by how fast humans can type. It is increasingly limited by how well humans can preserve structure.

What I learned building trading and AI systems on Wall Street

I have spent more than 20 years building trading systems for Wall Street, and over that time I have also built AI systems in that environment, often in places where performance, clarity, and reliability matter deeply.

These are not environments where architecture is an academic concern. They are environments where systems need to be fast, robust, understandable, and easy to evolve under real pressure.

Over the years, I have worked on electronic trading platforms, algorithmic trading systems, and AI-driven systems in finance, including machine learning models for stock prediction, risk analysis, and natural language processing. I have also worked on low-latency platforms where system behavior must remain highly predictable and enhancements must be introduced without destabilizing production workflows.

Across all of that work, I kept running into the same lesson:

The most maintainable large systems are the ones where execution flow is explicit.

Not implied. Not buried. Not scattered across conventions.

Explicit.

That is one of the reasons I have become such a strong believer in pipelines as a software architecture model.

Most systems are already pipelines. They just do not admit it.

If you step back, a surprising number of systems are really just sequences of actions.

  • Validate

  • Enrich

  • Transform

  • Call an external service

  • Apply rules

  • Persist

  • Notify

  • Observe

That is true for backend services. It is true for trading systems. It is true for AI workflows. It is true for orchestration logic. It is true for agents and decision systems.

The pattern is everywhere.

But in many codebases, that flow is not modeled directly. Instead, it is spread across the system in ad hoc ways. One part is in a service class. Another is in a utility. Another is hidden in a wrapper. Another is embedded in retry logic. Another is mixed into some framework callback or lifecycle hook.

The result is that the structure exists, but it is implicit.

And when structure is implicit, maintainability suffers.

A concrete example: system alerts

Consider a system alerts platform.

An event arrives from the market, from order flow, from a risk feed, or from an internal signal. That event may need to be normalized, enriched with account or instrument context, evaluated against rules or a model, checked for severity, and then published to the right downstream systems.

In many codebases, that flow gets buried across service classes, callback handlers, framework glue, retry wrappers, and utility methods. The logic may work, but the architecture becomes harder to see every month.

A pipeline makes the same flow explicit.

​x
preActions
  subscribe to the right inputs
  normalize the incoming event envelope
  attach correlation, instrument, or account context


actions
  enrich the event
  run scoring, rules, or model inference
  short-circuit if the alert is not actionable
  determine severity and routing


postActions
  publish to the right outputs
  emit metrics and audit records
  notify downstream systems or dashboards

This matters because preActions and postActions are not just technical hooks. They create reusable templates around the core business logic.

For example, an alerts platform might use common preActions to subscribe to specific inputs, attach run metadata, and normalize payloads before every pipeline begins. It might use common postActions to publish alerts to specific outputs, emit metrics, persist audit records, and notify downstream consumers after the decision has been made. Even when an alert short-circuits because it is not actionable, those postActions can still handle the operational work that should always happen.

That means each pipeline does not need to reinvent the outer mechanics every time.

It can focus on the thing that actually changes.

The actions.

And that is a very powerful place to be when large systems need to evolve quickly.

Why pipelines improve maintainability

A pipeline forces structure.

It takes a system that might otherwise feel scattered and gives it a visible, composable flow. Instead of trying to infer what the system is doing by reading through layers of indirection, you can look at the pipeline and immediately see the story.

  • What happens first?

  • What happens next?

  • What can short-circuit?

  • What always runs?

  • Where does a remote call happen?

  • Where is the model invoked?

  • Where is the audit step?

  • Where are the metrics captured?

The flow becomes obvious.

That kind of clarity changes how teams build software.

Adding a feature becomes much simpler because it increasingly becomes a question of:

What action am I adding?

That is a very different development experience from asking:

  • Which service should own this?

  • Which abstraction layer should this go through?

  • Which helper already partially does this?

  • Where is the safest place to wire this in?

  • What other part of the system might this accidentally affect?

When architecture is pipeline-oriented, enhancement becomes more deliberate and more local. The unit of change is clear.

Why this matters even more with AI-generated code

This is where I think the topic becomes especially important.

AI is very good at generating code.

Generating code is not the same thing as preserving architecture.

Without structure, AI can produce systems that are harder to understand over time, even if each individual piece of code looks reasonable. It can accelerate output while also accelerating inconsistency.

What teams need is not just AI-assisted coding.

They need AI-assisted coding inside an architecture that humans can still reason about.

That is a very different goal.

A pipeline can act as a forcing function for that structure.

Instead of letting generated code spread unpredictably through a system, you can constrain it into explicit actions with clear input and output behavior. You can make the flow visible. You can review it more easily. You can test it more directly. You can evolve it without losing the plot.

In other words, AI should not just help us write code faster.

It should help us build systems that stay maintainable.

Local clarity first. Distribution only when it is actually needed.

One of the architectural tensions I have seen repeatedly over the years is the tradeoff between monoliths and microservices.

Monoliths can become rigid if structure is poor.

Microservices can become fragmented if distribution is overused.

A lot of teams discovered the hard way that distributing a system too early introduces serious costs: operational overhead, debugging complexity, versioning friction, deployment coordination, latency, reliability concerns, and data consistency challenges.

What many systems really need is not immediate distribution.

They need clear internal composition.

That is another reason I believe in pipeline-oriented design. It gives you modularity and explicit flow without requiring you to fragment the system prematurely. You can keep execution local when locality is the better design. You can distribute only when distribution is genuinely warranted.

That is a much healthier default.

The architecture should read like a story

One of the best tests for a large system is simple:

Can a developer understand its main behavior without reading half the codebase?

A pipeline makes that much more realistic.

A well-structured pipeline can read almost like a narrative:

  • validate input

  • normalize data

  • enrich context

  • run risk checks

  • call a pricing service

  • apply business rules

  • persist result

  • emit metrics

  • notify downstream consumers

That kind of clarity is not just nice to have. It directly affects delivery speed.

When the architecture reads clearly, teams can:

  • add new actions faster

  • isolate failures more easily

  • review changes with more confidence

  • onboard engineers more quickly

  • keep systems healthier over time

This is especially important in complex domains like finance, where systems often live for years, requirements evolve continuously, and correctness alone is never enough.

Why I built Pipeline Services

These ideas are what led me to build Pipeline Services, an open-source, locality-aware software architecture framework for composing application behavior as typed, observable pipelines of actions.

Its purpose is not cloud orchestration or deployment infrastructure.

Its purpose is application architecture.

At the center of the model is a simple but powerful structure:

preActions -> actions -> postActions

That structure makes room for local actions, remote actions, and even prompt-directed actions that can be compiled into normal, reviewable code.

The goal is to make flow explicit and make systems easier to understand, maintain, and evolve.

In practice, that changes how enhancement work feels.

You are no longer hunting through scattered logic wondering where a new feature belongs.

More often than not, the question becomes:

What action am I adding?

That question is simple, but it is powerful. It turns large-system enhancement into a much clearer design conversation: what behavior am I introducing, where does it belong in the flow, and should it live in preActions, actions, or postActions?

That is the kind of architectural simplicity that scales.

Maintainability is now a competitive advantage

For a long time, speed of development was treated as the main advantage.

Now that AI is changing what speed even means, I think the real advantage is shifting.

The winners will not just be the teams that generate code fastest.

They will be the teams that preserve clarity while generating code fast.

They will be the teams that can continue adding features quickly six months later, one year later, two years later, because the underlying system still makes sense.

That is what maintainability really is. It is not about making software feel neat. It is about protecting your future rate of progress.

The more code AI helps us produce, the more important that becomes.

Final thought

I am deeply optimistic about AI and what it means for software engineering.

But I do not think the future belongs to unstructured code generation.

I think it belongs to systems where AI helps create software inside an architecture that remains visible, understandable, and easy to change.

For me, pipelines have been one of the most effective ways to achieve that.

Not because they make systems simpler in a superficial sense, but because they make complexity easier to organize, reason about, and extend.

And in the years ahead, that may matter more than anything else.
